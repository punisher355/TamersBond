# Chapter 6: Skills

A skill is a trained, specific application of a Crest Stat: the difference between being Courageous in general and being specifically good at vaulting a fence without breaking stride. Where a Crest Stat governs the big combat math (hit rate, damage, HP, and so on), skills are what let a Tamer act on everything else: reading a room, hacking a terminal, patching up an ally, standing their ground when everything is going wrong.

Every skill starts at rank 1 for free, same as your stats. There are 24 of them in total, each tied to one of the six Crest Stats, and each one runs from rank 1 up to a cap of rank 6.

> **The Stat Cap Rule**
>
> Through rank 5, a skill's rank can never exceed its parent Crest Stat's rank. If your Friendship is rank 3, none of your Friendship skills can go above rank 3 either. You have to raise the stat before you can raise the skills underneath it.
>
> Rank 6, the top of the scale, is a special case. Pushing a skill all the way to rank 6 requires its parent Crest Stat to be at rank 9, not just rank 6. True mastery of a skill asks a lot more of the underlying stat than the normal step-by-step climb does.

## Making a Skill Check

When a skill applies to what your Tamer is trying to do, here's the roll: grab a number of d6 equal to your rank in that skill, roll them all, and add them together. A rank 3 skill rolls 3d6. A rank 6 skill, the maximum, rolls 6d6.

The GM looks at what you're attempting and sets a Difficulty Number, or DN, based on how hard the fiction says the task actually is. Add up your dice and compare the total to the DN. Meet or beat it and you succeed. Fall short and you don't.

Because your result comes from adding up a pile of dice instead of one die plus a flat bonus, a single point of skill rank matters more here than it would elsewhere. Going from rank 2 to rank 3 doesn't just nudge your average result up a little; it hands you a whole extra die, which shifts both how high you can roll and how reliably you land near your average.

As a guide for setting a DN:

| Difficulty | DN | This is roughly what a rank... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine | 6 | ...rank 2 skill clears comfortably, and even rank 1 has a real shot at |
| Moderate | 10 | ...rank 3 skill clears about 6 times in 10 |
| Hard | 14 | ...rank 4 skill clears about half the time |
| Very Hard | 18 | ...rank 5 skill clears about half the time |
| Extreme | 22 | ...rank 6 skill clears less than half the time, even at the cap |

> **Worth Knowing: The Odds by Rank**
>
> Because the dice add up instead of scaling a flat bonus, the odds move fast between ranks. Here's the actual success chance at each Difficulty, rank by rank:
>
> | Rank | Dice | Routine (6) | Moderate (10) | Hard (14) | Very Hard (18) | Extreme (22) |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | 1 | 1d6 | 17% | 0% | 0% | 0% | 0% |
> | 2 | 2d6 | 72% | 17% | 0% | 0% | 0% |
> | 3 | 3d6 | 95% | 63% | 16% | 1% | 0% |
> | 4 | 4d6 | ~100% | 90% | 56% | 16% | 1% |
> | 5 | 5d6 | ~100% | 98% | 85% | 50% | 15% |
> | 6 | 6d6 | ~100% | ~100% | 96% | 79% | 45% |
>
> One thing worth knowing before you set a DN in play: at rank 1, anything harder than Routine is genuinely impossible, not just unlikely. A single d6 can't roll higher than 6, so a rank 1 skill can never reach a Moderate DN of 10 no matter how the dice land. That's a real consequence of adding dice together against a fixed number, not a rounding error, so keep it in mind when you're tempted to call for a Hard check from someone who's barely trained. If that hard floor feels wrong once you've played with it, the fix is to shift this whole table down rather than to raise it, since the ceiling side (ranks 5 and 6 struggling against Extreme) is doing what it should.

The GM doesn't need to call for a skill check every time a character does something related to a skill. If the task is simple enough to succeed automatically, just let it happen.

### Helping Someone

Sometimes more than one Tamer wants to throw effort at the same task instead of just one person going it alone. When that happens, name one Tamer the Primary; theirs is the check that actually decides whether the task succeeds or fails. Everyone else pitching in is Helping.

Each Helping Tamer rolls the same skill the Primary is using, but against a DN one tier lower than the Primary is facing (a Hard task becomes a Moderate check to help with, and so on). If a Helper meets or beats that lower DN, the Primary's own DN drops by 2 before they roll. If a Helper falls short, the Primary's DN rises by 2 instead; fumbling the help makes the job harder, not easier. Multiple Helpers stack, for better or worse.

None of this changes whose roll actually decides the outcome. The task succeeds or fails entirely on the Primary Tamer's check against their DN, adjusted for whatever help they got. A Helper who completely blows their roll doesn't sink the task on its own if the Primary still clears their DN, and a Helper who succeeds cleanly doesn't guarantee anything either if the Primary still falls short.

> The exact numbers here, a Helper DN one tier down and a swing of 2 per Helper, are a starting point picked to line up cleanly with the DN table above. Adjust them if they don't feel right once you've run it at the table.

### Skill EXP Costs

Skills cost less EXP to raise than Crest Stats, and the table now stops at rank 6 instead of rank 10:

| Upgrade | EXP Cost | Running Total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rank 1 → 2 | 100 | 100 |
| Rank 2 → 3 | 150 | 250 |
| Rank 3 → 4 | 200 | 450 |
| Rank 4 → 5 | 250 | 700 |
| Rank 5 → 6 | 300 | 1,000 |

Remember that the Stat Cap Rule still applies on top of this cost. Paying the EXP for rank 6 doesn't do anything until the parent Crest Stat has also reached rank 9. You can bank the rank if you've got the EXP for it early, but it won't do anything for you until the stat catches up.

## Looking Ahead

One more piece of a Tamer rounds out their sheet, and it's getting its own full chapter rather than a paragraph here.

**Your Partner** is that last piece: choosing your Digimon's line and building out their own sheet using their own separate 1,500 EXP. That starts in Chapter 7: Who Is Your Digimon?

If you haven't spent any EXP toward a class yet, that's completely normal. Between your Crest Stat and your skills, the starting 1,500 EXP disappears fast. Classes are covered back in Chapter 4, and there's nothing wrong with entering play with EXP still unspent; you can put it toward a class the moment you've earned enough.

## Example: Building a Tamer

Here's the whole process in one pass, using a Tamer named Kaito and his 1,500 starting EXP.

**Who Are You.** Kaito is 12, sharp-tongued, and more comfortable behind a screen than in a crowd. His Want is to prove he's useful without a keyboard in front of him; his Fear is being right about something and having nobody listen anyway. His Crest is Knowledge.

**Crest Stats.** Kaito puts 900 EXP into Knowledge, taking it from rank 1 to rank 4. That's the bulk of his budget gone, but it's also his Crest, and it raises the ceiling on every Knowledge skill he owns. He leaves his other five stats at rank 1 for now. 600 EXP remain.

Because Kaito has earned 1,500 total EXP so far, his Hope Pool is 20, straight off the table in Chapter 5. It doesn't matter that most of it went into a single stat; total EXP earned is all that counts.

**Skills.** With Knowledge at rank 4, Kaito can raise any Knowledge skill as high as rank 4. He takes Decode to rank 3 (250 EXP) and Modify to rank 2 (100 EXP), spending 350 EXP and leaving 250 remaining.

**Classes.** 250 EXP isn't enough to open a class row from any book Kaito's group is using, and that's fine and expected at creation. He holds onto it. Thanks to the new Hope rule, saving it here costs him nothing.

**Your Partner.** Kaito's GM helps him settle on a Rookie-line partner suited to a Knowledge-leaning Tamer, and they move to Chapter 7 to finish building her.

Kaito walks away from creation with Knowledge 4, Decode 3, Modify 2, everything else at rank 1, a Hope Pool of 20, 16 HP, and 250 EXP still sitting in reserve for whatever Session 1 throws at him.
